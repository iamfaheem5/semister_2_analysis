### 2020 — Literature & Philosophy

**Q1**
(a) Literature deals imaginatively with the stuff life is made of — human thoughts, feelings, experiences expressed through familiar matter or fantasy.
(b) Earlier literature & philosophy formed the basis of education; now science is indispensable but literature still holds privileged position.
(c) Literature humanises knowledge, gives personal, distinct personality-based experience of life, cultivates imagination and human values.
(d) Theme: Co-existence and complementary roles of literature and science in modern education; literature's enduring humanising value.
(e) The passage instigates a harmonious/complementary relation — no inherent enmity; both important, science prime but literature equally valuable. (Note phrasing "What type of relation does the passage instigate in the passage?" is rhetorical — answer: complementary/harmonious.)

**Q2**
(a) indispensable — absolutely necessary, cannot do without.
(b) privileged — having special advantage/status.
(c) distinct — clearly different/recognizable, unique.
(d) enmity — hostility, antagonism, ill-will.
(e) fantasy — imaginative, unreal creation.

**Q3 — Précis (80 words) — Title: Literature and Science: Complementary Pillars**
Earlier education rested on literature and philosophy. With science becoming indispensable, debate arose over literature's place. While science deserves prime importance in modern curricula, literature remains equally vital and not antagonistic to science. Literature imaginatively portrays human thought and feeling through real or fanciful matters, offering personal contact with distinct personalities. Science generalizes impersonal universal principles. Literature's humanised, personal viewpoint complements science's objectivity.

### 2021 — Good Governance

**Q1**
a) Central idea: Good governance is a model for evaluating public institutions on human rights, rule of law, corruption-free management, and its application varies by context.
b) Factors: conduct of public affairs, management of public resources, realization of human rights, absence of abuse/corruption, rule of law, decision-making and implementation process.
c) Responsibility of government/governing bodies: to meet needs of the masses (not select groups), ensure effective, viable governance.
d) Aid organizations/developed countries tend to impose their own agenda-based requirements, measuring other states against liberal-democratic (Europe/Americas) standards, so "good governance" becomes context-dependent.
e) Good governance essentially means effective, accountable, participatory, rule-of-law-based governance that serves the many.

**Q2**
viable — capable of succeeding/sustainable; measure — to assess/judge; agenda — plan/priorities; emerge — to come into being/appear; requirement — necessary condition.

**Q3 Précis — Title: The Many Meanings of Good Governance**
Good governance measures how institutions manage public affairs and human rights without corruption, respecting the rule of law. It applies at all levels. It serves as a model contrasting effective and ineffective states, emphasizing government's duty to the masses. Since liberal democracies are seen as most successful, they set the benchmark. Aid agencies often redefine good governance to suit their agendas, making it context-specific. (70 words)

### 2022 — Internationalization

**Q1(a)**
i. c) Paradigm alteration (transcendence of boundaries, seismic shift)
ii. c) Equitable access (explicit challenge)
iii. c) Antagonism — language barriers antagonize diversity push (they are opposing tension requiring resolution)
iv. c) Fostering intercultural interactions (plus policies, but option c best)
v. b) Pragmatic acknowledgement (nuanced approach, not unbridled capitalism)

**Q1(b)**
i. Core concept: transcendence of geographic/cultural boundaries via partnerships, diverse student/scholar exchange and collaborative research for knowledge enrichment.
ii. Challenges: equitable access, maintaining quality in diverse environment, language barriers, integration of international students, commodification of knowledge.
iii. Diversity promotion requires addressing barriers so students from different linguistic backgrounds can engage in discourse.
iv. Improved via institutional/governmental policies, intercultural interaction, language support, inclusive pedagogy.
v. Concern: profit motive may undermine educational mission (commodification). Address by nuanced, vigilant approach balancing benefits and mission.

**Q1(c) Summary — Title: Internationalization: Promise and Pitfalls**
Internationalization transforms higher education by fostering global partnerships and multicultural cross-pollination to tackle modern challenges. It promotes diversity and cross-cultural understanding. However, it faces challenges of equitable access, quality maintenance, language barriers, student integration and commercialization. Institutions must adopt a balanced, nuanced approach that maximizes benefits while addressing challenges. (55 words)

### 2023 — Philadelphia Murals

**Q1(a)** i.a, ii.b, iii.d, iv.d, v.d

**Q1(b)**
i. Jane Golden, New Jersey artist.
ii. Nearly 3,000.
iii. Learned collaboration, getting along with diverse people, responsibility/schedule-following, pride in community, artistic skills.
iv. Across the city — poorest neighborhoods and affluent ones; special buses take tourists to different parts.
v. Nation's largest public art program and model for other cities helping troubled youth.

**Q1(c) Summary — Title: Philadelphia's Mural Program**
Philadelphia, first US capital and signer of Declaration, is famed for ~3,000 murals. Artist Jane Golden's program 20+ years ago paired troubled youth with artists, teaching collaboration, responsibility and community pride while creating art. Murals beautify poor and affluent neighborhoods alike, attract tourists via bus tours, and have become the country's largest public art program and a national model. (60 words)
